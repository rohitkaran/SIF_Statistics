The SEBI regulatory framework for SIFs

Specialized Investment Funds are a new, distinct category created by SEBI to give informed investors access to sophisticated, long-short strategies inside a regulated, pooled structure. This is a plain-language guide to the rules that govern them.

SIFintel · Education

Why SEBI created SIFs

For years, Indian investors sat with a gap in the middle of the market. On one side were mutual funds, tightly regulated and accessible from a few hundred rupees, but limited in the strategies they could run. On the other were Portfolio Management Services (PMS) and Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), which allow more sophisticated approaches but come with high entry thresholds and, historically, uneven investor protection.

Into that gap crept a number of loosely regulated or mis-sold products promising hedge-fund-style returns. SEBI's response was to build a formal category that sits between mutual funds and PMS/AIFs: the Specialized Investment Fund, or SIF. The goal is twofold — give genuinely informed investors regulated access to newer strategies (including long-short approaches), and curb the unregulated products that had been filling the vacuum.

The legal basis and timeline

SIFs are not a wholly separate law. SEBI created the category by amending the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996, and then issuing a detailed framework circular in February 2025. The framework was broadly made effective from 1 April 2025.

Crucially, although SIFs live under the mutual fund regulations, they are treated as a distinct product — separate from mutual funds, and separate again from PMS and AIFs. They borrow the operational discipline of the mutual fund world (custody, daily NAV, disclosure norms) while permitting strategies that ordinary mutual funds cannot run.

Who can launch a SIF: the two eligibility routes

Not every asset management company (AMC) can offer a SIF. SEBI set two alternative routes to qualify, so that both large incumbents and capable specialists can participate.

RouteCore requirement
EstablishedA mutual fund that has been operating for at least 3 years, with average AUM of at least ₹10,000 crore over the last 3 years, and a clean regulatory record.
New / alternateAppoint a Chief Investment Officer and an additional fund manager who each meet SEBI's specified experience and AUM-managed thresholds.

Under either route, the AMC must notify SEBI and satisfy the applicable conditions before it launches a SIF. The two-track design is deliberate: it does not restrict SIFs to only the very largest houses, provided the right investment talent and controls are in place.

Branding and separation rules

Because a SIF sits so close to a mutual fund operationally, SEBI is strict about keeping the two visibly apart. A SIF must carry a distinct brand name, separate from the parent mutual fund, with its own identity, its own website or clearly demarcated section, and prominent risk disclaimers.

The purpose is to prevent an investor from confusing a higher-risk SIF strategy with a familiar, lower-risk mutual fund scheme simply because they share a sponsor's name. The separation is about informed consent: an investor should always know when they have stepped from the mutual fund world into the SIF world.

The ₹10 lakh minimum and the accredited-investor exemption

The single most important gate on who can invest is the minimum ticket size. A SIF requires a minimum investment of ₹10 lakh per investor. This threshold is aggregated across all SIF strategies of a given AMC and measured at the PAN level — an investor cannot spread smaller amounts across several strategies to slip under the bar.

There is one notable exemption. Accredited investors — those who meet SEBI's wealth and income criteria and hold valid accreditation — are exempt from the ₹10 lakh minimum. The logic is that the threshold exists to ensure only investors who can absorb the risk participate, and accreditation already establishes that capacity.

The ₹10 lakh floor is a suitability filter, not a fee or a lock-in. It is designed to keep SIF strategies in the hands of investors who understand and can bear the higher risk these products carry.

Permitted strategies and the one-per-category rule

SIFs are not a licence to run anything. SEBI has defined a closed set of seven permitted investment strategies, grouped across three buckets — Equity, Debt and Hybrid:

  • Equity: Equity Long-Short; Equity Ex-Top-100 Long-Short; Sector Rotation Long-Short.
  • Debt: Debt Long-Short; Sectoral Debt Long-Short.
  • Hybrid: Active Asset Allocator Long-Short; Hybrid Long-Short.

A key constraint governs how many an AMC may run: only one strategy per category per AMC. This keeps a house from launching multiple near-identical products in the same bucket, reduces investor confusion, and encourages each strategy to be genuinely differentiated.

Short-selling and derivative limits

The defining feature of these strategies is the "long-short" element — the ability to profit from falling prices as well as rising ones. SEBI permits this, but on tight terms. A SIF may take short exposure only through exchange-traded derivatives; it cannot short physical securities directly.

There is also a cap on how much directional downside bet a SIF can carry. The maximum unhedged short exposure taken through derivatives is limited (SEBI's limit is around 25% of net assets, as specified by SEBI). This ensures the fund's short book stays within controlled bounds rather than becoming an unbounded speculative position.

Structure, liquidity and disclosures

A SIF can be structured as open-ended, close-ended or interval, depending on the strategy. Because some long-short strategies need stable capital and cannot be unwound instantly, the subscription and redemption frequency is set in the offer document and may be less frequent than daily. Investors must read this carefully — liquidity is not guaranteed to match that of an ordinary open-ended mutual fund.

On transparency, SIFs inherit strong disclosure obligations:

  • Daily NAV, so investors can always value their holding.
  • Regular portfolio disclosures, typically on a monthly basis.
  • A scheme information / strategy document setting out how the strategy works and its risks.
  • Risk-band and risk disclosures, plus standard SEBI investor-protection norms.

What it means for investors

For an investor, the framework is best read as a set of trade-offs made explicit. SIFs open the door to strategies previously reachable only through PMS, AIFs or unregulated products — but they wrap them in mutual-fund-grade custody, NAV and disclosure. In exchange, the ₹10 lakh minimum, the distinct branding, the risk bands and the possibly reduced liquidity are all signals that these are not everyday products.

The right way to approach a SIF is to treat the regulatory guardrails as information. The separation rules tell you this is a different risk category; the strategy document tells you exactly what the manager can and cannot do; and the derivative limits tell you how far the fund can lean into a bet. Understanding each of these before investing is the whole point of the framework.
